Family Rules Printable (Free House Rules for Kids)

Bottom line: keep family rules short, say them in positive words, and write them together. Five to seven rules is plenty. Post them where kids can see them, decide the consequences ahead of time, and review them once a week so they stay real.

Why write family rules down?

Every family already has rules. Most of them live in a parent's head and come out loud, in the moment, after something has gone wrong. Writing them down changes that. Kids know what is expected before the trouble starts, both parents say the same thing, and you can point to the poster instead of giving the same speech again.

What makes a good family rule

  • Short. A 5-year-old should be able to say it back. We speak kindly, not a paragraph about tone.
  • Positive. Say what to do. We keep our hands gentle works better than no hitting, because it tells kids what to do instead.
  • Few. Five to seven rules. A list of twenty is a list nobody keeps.
  • Kept by everyone. Parents included. If the rule is no phones at meals, that means your phone too.
  • Clear about why. Older kids keep rules they understand. One sentence of reason is enough.

7 family rules to start with

  1. We tell the truth.
  2. We speak kindly.
  3. We keep our hands gentle.
  4. We listen the first time.
  5. We clean up our own messes.
  6. We say sorry and forgive.
  7. We help each other.

These cover most of what goes wrong in a house with young kids: lying, unkind words, hitting, ignoring instructions, mess, and grudges. Change the words to fit your family.

House rules ideas for kids

Pick from these for specific rooms or times of day. They work best as a second, shorter list under the main family rules, or on a picture card for little ones.

  • Respect: ask before you take, knock on closed doors, wait your turn to talk, no name calling, say please and thank you.
  • Home: shoes off at the door, coats and bags on hooks, put things back where they live, clean up before the next thing, inside voices inside.
  • Meals: eat at the table, try one bite, clear your own plate, no phones at meals.
  • Screens: screens in shared rooms, screens off at the set time, homework and chores before screens.
  • Safety: tell a grown-up where you go, seatbelts on before we drive, stay with a grown-up in a parking lot.
  • Bedtime: stay in bed after lights out, one drink and one trip to the bathroom.

Family rules by age

Ages 2 to 3: three rules at most, with pictures. Gentle hands, clean up toys, stay with a grown-up. Toddlers learn rules by being helped to keep them, so show the rule and then do it with them.

Ages 4 to 6: four or five rules in short, positive words. Practice when everyone is calm. Act out the right way and the wrong way and let them catch you. Picture cards help kids who cannot read yet.

Ages 7 to 9: five to seven rules, with the reason for each. Let them help write the list and sign it. They notice fairness, so make sure the rules apply to everyone.

Ages 10 to 12: add rules for screens, friends, and time away from home. Ask them to suggest one rule and one consequence. You keep the final say, but kids push back less on rules they helped write.

Consequences that teach

Decide consequences ahead of time, while nobody is upset. The best ones connect to the rule.

  • Broke it, fix it. A mess gets cleaned up. A broken toy gets repaired or replaced from allowance.
  • Lose the thing that was misused. Screens past the time limit means no screens tomorrow.
  • Make it right with the person. Unkind words mean a real apology and one kind act.
  • Reset, then try again. For little kids, a short sit with a grown-up and a do-over beats a long punishment.

Keep it calm and keep it consistent. A small consequence every time teaches more than a big one now and then.

How to make your family rules together

  1. Pick a calm time. A weekend family meeting works well, not right after a fight.
  2. Ask what makes our home good to live in. Write down everything kids say.
  3. Group and trim. Combine ideas into 5 to 7 short, positive rules.
  4. Add the why. One sentence for each rule.
  5. Agree on consequences. Use the make-it-right chart.
  6. Sign it. Everyone signs, parents first.
  7. Post it at kid height. On the fridge, by the door, or in the kitchen.
  8. Review it weekly. Celebrate a rule that is going well and pick one to work on.

Common mistakes

  • Too many rules. Start with five. Add one only when another is working.
  • Only negative words. Kids hear the action, not the no. Say what to do.
  • Rules for kids only. Kids watch whether parents keep them.
  • Consequences invented in anger. They tend to be too big, and you end up taking them back.
  • Post it and forget it. A rule list that is never mentioned becomes wallpaper.
