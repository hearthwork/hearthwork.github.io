Morning Routine for Preschoolers (Free Printable Chart With Pictures)

Bottom line: a preschool morning routine works with 5 or 6 picture steps in the same order every day, a visible clock or timer, and you in the room for the first few weeks. Make the chart, put it at kid height, and let the pictures do the reminding.

What should a preschooler's morning routine include?

Five or six steps is the limit for ages 3 to 5. More than that and the chart becomes a wall. A solid order for most families:

  1. Wake up and potty.
  2. Get dressed. Lay clothes out the night before.
  3. Breakfast.
  4. Brush teeth and hair.
  5. Shoes and coat on.
  6. Backpack by the door. Only if you need it.

Put the hardest step early, while your child still has patience. For many 4 year olds that is getting dressed. Put the fun step last, like a book or a song in the car, so the end of the routine feels like a reward.

How long should a preschool morning routine take?

Plan on 45 to 60 minutes from waking to out the door. A 3 year old needs closer to an hour, and a 5 year old can often do it in 40 minutes. Preschoolers have no sense of time, so a sand timer or a simple visual timer helps more than saying "hurry up." Start the routine 10 minutes earlier than you think you need. A buffer removes most of the yelling.

Sample preschool morning schedule

  • 7:00 Wake up, potty, hug.
  • 7:10 Get dressed (clothes picked the night before).
  • 7:25 Breakfast.
  • 7:50 Brush teeth and hair.
  • 8:00 Shoes, coat, backpack.
  • 8:10 Out the door, or free play if you are home.

Shift the times to fit your day. The order matters more than the clock.

Why pictures work better than words

Most preschoolers cannot read yet. A picture routine chart shows the next step without you repeating it. You can point to the picture instead of saying "Did you brush your teeth?" for the fifth time. Kids also like to move a card or check a box, which gives them a small sense of control. How to set up a morning routine for a preschooler

  1. Pick 5 or 6 steps with your child. Let them choose the order of one or two steps.
  2. Prep the night before. Clothes on the chair, shoes by the door, backpack packed. Most morning fights are really missing-shoe fights.
  3. Practice on a weekend. Walk through it slowly with a game attitude. Nobody is late on Saturday.
  4. Stay close for 2 to 3 weeks. At 3 and 4, the routine is done with you, not by you. Narrate, point to the chart, and step back as they get faster.
  5. Hang the chart at kid height on the bedroom door or in the bathroom.
  6. Praise the process. "You got dressed and brushed teeth before breakfast" beats "good job."

What to do when a preschooler will not cooperate

  • Dawdling. Use a timer and make it a race against the timer, not against you. Do not rush the step you are on, just shorten the gaps.
  • Refusing to get dressed. Offer two outfits, both already approved by you. Choice ends most battles.
  • Tantrums over the routine. Stay calm, name the step ("Next is shoes"), and keep moving. Save the talk for after.
  • Dragging on breakfast. Set a timer for 20 minutes and take the plate when it rings.
  • Getting distracted by toys. No toys until the routine is done. It is the reward, not the obstacle.

Common mistakes

  • Too many steps. Six is the ceiling.
  • Changing the order every day. Same order, same time, or the chart is useless.
  • Doing it for them when you are running late. One bad morning is fine. A habit of rescuing is not.
  • Screens in the morning. They make every step after harder. Keep the TV off until the routine is done, or skip it entirely on school days.
  • Rewards that are too big. A sticker, a song, or a high five is enough.
