Thanksgiving Gratitude Activities for Kids (Free Printable)

Bottom line: gratitude sticks with kids when it is small, daily, and done together. Start a thankful tree or a thankful list on November 1, play a gratitude dice game at dinner once or twice a week, and set a table talk card at each plate on Thanksgiving, Thursday, November 26.

Why gratitude activities help kids

Kids do not start out saying thank you without a prompt. They learn it by naming good things out loud, again and again, with the people they love. A short activity gives them the words. Doing it every day for a few weeks turns it into a habit that lasts past the holiday.

8 simple Thanksgiving gratitude activities

  • Thankful tree. Set a bare branch in a jar of rocks and add one paper leaf a day with something each person is thankful for. A paper tree taped to the wall works too.
  • Gratitude dice game. Roll a die at dinner and answer the prompt for that number. It works for a 3-year-old and a 12-year-old at the same table.
  • Thankful list on the fridge. One line a day through November. By Thanksgiving you have 26 things to read back together.
  • Gratitude jar. Write one thing on a slip of paper each night and drop it in a jar. Read them all aloud on Thanksgiving Day.
  • Thank-you notes. Each child writes or draws one note to a teacher, coach, grandparent, or neighbor. Deliver it by hand if you can.
  • Table talk cards. Set a question card at each plate on Thanksgiving so the conversation goes past pass the rolls.
  • Gratitude walk. Walk around the block and name five things you see that you are glad for.
  • Thankful prayer. At bedtime, each person thanks God for one specific thing from the day. Specific beats general: the soup Grandma made, not just food.

Gratitude activities by age

Ages 2 to 4: keep it concrete. They can color a leaf, point at a picture, and say one word. Ask what made you happy today instead of what are you thankful for.

Ages 5 to 8: they can write or dictate a sentence. Let them roll the dice, tape leaves to the tree, and draw a thank-you card. Ask them why, not just what.

Ages 9 to 12: push past my family and my house. Ask them to name a hard thing that turned out good, a person who helped them this year, or something they usually take for granted. They can write real thank-you notes and lead the dinner prompt.

How to play the gratitude dice game

  1. Cut out the paper die from the printable, fold on the dashed lines, and glue the tabs. Or borrow a die from any board game.
  2. Pass the die around the table. Each person rolls once.
  3. Answer the prompt for that number in one or two sentences.
  4. Anyone else can add a me too or ask one question.
  5. Play one round and stop while kids still want more.

The six prompts on our die:

  • 1: a person I am thankful for
  • 2: something that made me smile today
  • 3: a place I love to be
  • 4: something God made that I love
  • 5: a food I am glad we have
  • 6: something I learned this year

Thanksgiving table talk questions

  • What happened this year that you are glad about?
  • Who at this table are you thankful for, and why?
  • What hard thing this year turned out to be good?
  • What is your favorite Thanksgiving food, and who made it first?
  • What do you have now that you used to wish for?
  • Who helped you this year and does not know you are thankful?
  • What is a place you are thankful for?
  • If you could thank anyone in the world, who would it be?

A simple November gratitude plan

  1. November 1: set up the thankful tree or tape the thankful list to the fridge.
  2. Every night at dinner: add one leaf or one line. It takes under a minute.
  3. Once or twice a week: play one round of the gratitude dice game.
  4. Thanksgiving week: write thank-you notes and deliver them.
  5. Thanksgiving Day: set a table talk card at each plate and read the leaves or the list aloud before pie.

Starting late is fine. Start tonight and count from there.

Keep gratitude going after Thanksgiving

The habit fades fast once the leaves come down. The simplest fix is to give gratitude a fixed spot in the week. A short weekly family meeting that opens with each person sharing one appreciation does exactly that, and kids start to expect it.
