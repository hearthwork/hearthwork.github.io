Allowance Chart by Age (Free Printable Chore Chart With Money)

Bottom line: give a small, steady allowance on the same day every week, keep everyday family chores unpaid, and let kids earn extra for bigger jobs. Start around $1 per year of age per week, or half that, and split every payday into spend, save, and give.

Should allowance be tied to chores?

There are three common ways to do it.

  • Commission only: kids get paid for each chore. It teaches that work earns money, but kids can opt out of helping when they do not need cash.
  • Allowance only: kids get a set amount no matter what. It teaches budgeting, but chores and money are disconnected.
  • Base plus extras: a small set allowance for learning to manage money, everyday chores done because you live here, and extra paid jobs for kids who want more.

We recommend base plus extras. Nobody gets paid to clear their own plate, and a kid who wants a new book can wash the car to get there faster.

How much allowance by age

A common rule of thumb is about $1 per year of age per week. Many families give half that, and that works fine. What matters most is that it comes on the same day every week and that kids make real choices with it.

Ages 4 to 5: a few coins a week in clear jars so they can watch it grow. The lesson is simple. Money is for choosing, and once it is spent it is gone.

Ages 6 to 7: about $3 to $7 a week. Start the spend, save, and give split. Help them save for one small thing they want so they learn to wait.

Ages 8 to 9: about $5 to $9 a week. Add one or two paid extra jobs. Let them make a purchase you think is a waste. A disappointing $6 toy is a cheap lesson.

Ages 10 to 12: about $8 to $12 a week. Hand over a few small costs they used to ask you for, like snacks with friends or game add-ons. Now it is a budget, not just pocket money.

Chores that stay unpaid

These are the family chores. Everyone does them because everyone lives here.

  • Make your bed and put away your clothes.
  • Clear your plate and help set the table.
  • Pick up toys and your things from shared rooms.
  • Help with the weekly reset, like a 15-minute family tidy on Saturday.

Extra jobs kids can earn money for

  • Washing the car or vacuuming the car seats.
  • Weeding a garden bed or raking a section of the yard.
  • Washing windows or wiping down baseboards.
  • Sorting the garage shelf or the toy bins.
  • Helping cook a full dinner, start to finish.

Set the price before the job starts, and pay when a grown-up has checked it. A half-done job earns nothing until it is finished.

Spend, save, give

Split every payday into three jars. A simple starting split is half to spend, a third to save, and the rest to give. Let kids pick what the give jar goes to, whether that is church, a food bank, or a neighbor in need. Clear jars beat a piggy bank for young kids because they can see the money grow.

Make payday work

  1. Pick one payday and stick to it. Sunday evening or Friday after school both work well.
  2. Pay in cash for kids under 9. Coins and dollar bills feel real in a way numbers do not.
  3. Post the chart on the fridge so nobody has to keep track in their head.
  4. Do not lend against next week. Running out before payday is the lesson.
  5. Raise the allowance on birthdays, along with one new responsibility.

If a chore gets skipped

Keep family chores and money separate. A skipped bed does not cost money, but it still has to get done before screens or play. For paid extra jobs, no work means no pay. That is enough consequence on its own.
