Winter Break Routine for Kids (Free Printable Schedule)

Bottom line: a good winter break routine is not a school schedule. Keep four anchors the same every day (wake-up, meals, a morning job, and bedtime), add one special thing, and leave the rest of the day open. Kids get rest and freedom, and you skip the 4 pm meltdown.

Why kids need a routine over winter break

Two weeks with no school sounds restful, but for most kids the first few days without a plan turn into late nights, constant snacking, and too many screens. Young children feel safest when they know what comes next. Older kids still do better with a little shape to the day. A light routine gives everyone that, without turning break into homeschool.

The four anchors

These stay the same every day of break, even on outing days.

  1. Wake-up. Within an hour of the usual school time. Sleeping in a little is a gift. Sleeping until 10 makes bedtime and the first day back hard.
  2. Meals at set times. Breakfast, lunch, and dinner at roughly the same time, with one planned snack. This alone cuts most of the grumpiness.
  3. A morning job. Get dressed, make the bed, and one small chore before any screens. It tells everyone the day has started.
  4. Bedtime. The same bedtime routine as school nights, or no more than 30 minutes later. See our morning and bedtime routine charts for a simple version.

Everything between the anchors can change day to day.

Sample winter break schedule

Ages 2 to 5

  • 7:00 wake up, get dressed, breakfast
  • 8:30 morning job and free play
  • 10:00 outside time, even 20 minutes in the cold
  • 11:30 lunch
  • 12:30 nap or quiet time with books
  • 2:30 one special thing: baking, a craft, the library
  • 4:00 free play
  • 5:30 dinner, bath, books, bed

Ages 6 to 12

  • 7:30 wake up, get dressed, make bed, breakfast
  • 8:30 morning job, then free time
  • 10:00 outside or active time
  • 12:00 lunch
  • 1:00 quiet hour: reading, drawing, Lego, a puzzle
  • 2:00 the day's special thing or a friend over
  • 4:00 screen window, if your family uses one
  • 6:00 dinner and family time
  • 8:00 bedtime routine

Treat the times as a guide. The order matters more than the clock.

Plan one special thing per day

Kids remember the cookies, the sledding, and the night you drove around to see the lights, not the whole day. Pick one thing per day and write it on a week-at-a-glance page. On a busy day with family or travel, the visit is the special thing. On a slow day, it can be as small as a pajama breakfast. If you are driving to see family, our screen-free road trip activities post has ideas for the car.

Winter break bucket list ideas

  • Build a snowman, go sledding, or take a winter walk
  • Bake cookies or bread together
  • Make paper snowflakes and a blanket fort
  • Do a big puzzle or learn a new card game
  • Go to the library and read a chapter book aloud
  • Drive around to see Christmas lights
  • Write thank-you notes and make a gift for a neighbor
  • Have a family game night and a pajama breakfast
  • Clean out the toy bins and donate what is outgrown
  • Plan the new year together at a family meeting

What about screen time over break?

Decide before break starts, not on the first morning. A simple rule works: screens come after the morning job and outside time, in one set window. Write it on the schedule so the chart says no, not you. If you want the whole family to agree on it, our free Screen-Time Agreement walks you through it in one sitting.

Use a picture schedule for young kids

Kids under 6 cannot read the plan, so show it. Put tomorrow's picture cards up the night before: breakfast, play outside, library, lunch, quiet time. In the morning they can see the day for themselves. Our guide to a visual schedule for kids explains how to set one up.

Ease back into school

Two days before school starts, go back to school wake-up and bedtime. Pack the backpack the night before the first day. The anchors you kept over break make this much easier.
